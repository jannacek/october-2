AT-LAB I was both an artistic work and a research intervention: an immersive audiovisual environment in which visitors’ own heartbeat and arm movement shaped light, image and spatial sound in real time. It was the first AT-LAB, run within the HLaS consortium at Stichting Landgoed de Camping, ’t Zandt.

The starting question was how participatory artistic experiences in technology might support digital health literacy: wearable self-tracking, sensors, and questions of control over one’s own data. Putting the participant’s body at the centre of both the aesthetic and the epistemic experience was a deliberate choice.

The poster presented at SAR25 reports two iterations from the first year and a half of the PD: 52 participants at ’t Zandt (2–4 June 2023) and 46 at Forum Groningen, the second iteration. Visitors completed a short two-page survey; the project description of June 2026 and the secondary analysis both count about fifty attendees at ’t Zandt, of whom 27 survey forms were retrievable. Attendance and analysed sample are kept apart on this page.

The installation

The environment

A custom-built space at Stichting Landgoed de Camping. Two interactive settings were set up: in the first, sensors collected movement and position data from the arms; in the second, heartbeat sensors fed back in real time. Software translated both streams into parameters of light, visuals and sound, including the spatialisation of sound on the x and y axes. Participants were audience and instrument at once.

The first iteration also used a volumetric display technique, which the poster credits with a more embodied interaction.

Evidence on file

Material
  • 27 retrievable two-page survey forms (54 pages), in Dutch: Likert items on comfort, confidence and satisfaction, plus open questions on “digital sovereignty”, the influence of the digital on daily life, and feedback
  • The poster also describes qualitative interviews and an autoethnographic view of art-making; the corpus analysed in 2026 is the survey forms
  • Installation documentation and photographs (see the Evidence Archive)
Why the corpus is modest
  • Gathered for a different primary purpose (digital sovereignty and health literacy), not designed to measure Perceived Agency
  • A self-selected convenience subset: 27 of roughly 50 attendees, no demographic data, no comparison condition
  • Cross-sectional: no before-and-after measure, so no claim about change or improvement

The 2026 secondary analysis New

In 2026 the 27 forms were re-read through the Perceived Agency lens, which was named only in 2026. The reading is retrospective and auditable: it shows what the lens makes legible in the earliest material, not that the installation produced agency.

The coding frame (codebook v0.2) was frozen before the full pass, every closed-item value was checked against the paper originals, anomalies were flagged rather than corrected away, and null cases were kept. A consistency check on 29 September 2026 (v1.1) applied two counting rules uniformly: borderline codings and generic expressions of enjoyment are recorded but not counted.

Counts are distinct respondents; categories overlap and do not form a scale. Borderline cases are recorded beside the counts but not included. They are traces in the material, not estimates of prevalence.

Entanglement is common; articulated control is rare. That gap between entanglement widely felt and control rarely put into words is the phenomenon the Perceived Agency lens was coined to name. It suggests that what is at stake may be less a knowledge deficit than a felt capacity to act, a question the responses raise rather than settle.

Unfamiliarity with the term and confidence with technology come apart. The evidence is at the level of the individual form, not of two separate distributions. Of the 18 respondents who say they do not know “digital sovereignty”, 7 rate their confidence with digital technology 4 or 5 (four of them 5: P01, P05, P11, P24), 5 rate it 3 and 6 rate it 1 or 2. The five who defined the term, or partly articulated it, all rate 4 or 5. High confidence is therefore found in both groups; low confidence appears only among those who did not articulate the term. This is a decoupling, not overconfidence, and with 27 forms and no demographic data it is a pattern in this corpus, not a finding about a population. Respondent-level values: E15 §A.1 and §B; reading in §E.2.

Concept language and control language overlap, partly by construction. The four respondents who framed sovereignty as ownership or control (P03, P13, P18 and P06) are the same people who defined the term or partly articulated it, and both codes are assigned from the same answers. So this is not independent evidence that vocabulary and a felt capacity to act depend on each other; it only shows that for the few who had words for the concept, those words were the language of control. The question stays open. E15 §E.3.

Agency appears where the scales cannot see it. Six people exercised judgement on the research itself, critiquing the survey or proposing how it could be redesigned. This is expressed agency: something participants said and did in the survey. It is different from the agency the installation offered (the freedom to move, the heartbeat as a controllable input), which was a designed opportunity; the two need different evidence and are kept apart on Boundary concepts.

Known limits of the analysis

One coder, who is also the artist-researcher and who developed the lens, so the codes may favour readings that confirm the framework. The frozen codebook and audit trail mitigate this but do not remove it; a delayed re-code of a subset, ideally by a second coder, is still to be done. Q8 and Q9 (comfort in the lab; would revisit, 24 yes, 1 no, 2 blank) show a positivity skew and are not read as evidence of agency. There was no member-checking, and the analysis says nothing about people who declined the survey.

A note on the SAR25 poster’s figures

The poster (May 2025) reports 14.8 % (4 of 27) as stating knowledge of the concept, and describes a “heightened sense of empowerment”. The 2026 re-coding with a frozen codebook distinguishes 3 who define the term and 2 partial articulations, and makes no claim that the installation increased agency. Figures on this site follow the 2026 analysis; the poster remains the record of the May 2025 reading.

What AT-LAB I supports

● Demonstrated
  • A biosensor installation was designed, built and run in two settings.
  • A 27-form corpus exists, verified against originals, with an auditable analysis.
  • Entanglement was the modal way respondents spoke of data; control was rarely articulated.
  • The work passed peer review as a poster (SAR25).
◆ Designed for
  • Building digital health literacy through participation (the original aim).
  • Reading Perceived Agency back into earlier material, as a lens for later labs.
! Not supported
  • Any claim of change, improvement or empowerment: the design is cross-sectional with no comparison.
  • Any claim about transfer to everyday life: it was not tested here.
  • Reciprocal influence on other HLaS work packages: technical collaboration is documented; changed questions elsewhere are not.

The lenses, as read in AT-LAB I

The three lenses were not set in advance, and none of them guided the design of this installation. They were named in February 2026, and Perceived Agency was read back into this material in the 2026 analysis. What follows is therefore a connection recognised later, not a decision taken at the time. The lenses are sensitising: they say where to look, not what will be found.

Concept Clarity
Read back into the corpus: only 5 of 27 articulated “digital sovereignty” (3 defined it, 2 partly), 18 did not know it. The lens is anchored empirically in AT-LAB II; here it raises a question about how vocabulary relates to a felt capacity to act.
Perceived Agency · anchor
The lens’s earliest traces: the gap between felt entanglement and articulated control, and agency showing up as embodied reaction and as judgement of the research itself.
Transferability
Not tested in AT-LAB I, in either sense. Nobody has re-run the installation (reuse of a format by another facilitator), and nothing shows participants applying what they took away to everyday life (application by participants). Participants mentioned everyday devices (smartwatches, phones, fitness trackers) unprompted, which suggests where an insight might be taken up, but nothing here shows that it was.

Feed-forward
