04 EMI Framework
Epistemic Mediation Indicators · practitioner-facing · exploratory

EMI Framework

Six dimensions for reflecting, from the inside, on what an artist-researcher contributes to a transdisciplinary collaboration. A working vocabulary, at version 0.3.

v0.3 · exploratory Not a validated instrument Article: in development

EMI is a practitioner-facing qualitative vocabulary for making the epistemic contribution of artistic research legible within transdisciplinary collaboration. It is not a metric, a quality standard or an impact checklist. It is a set of questions that help an artist-researcher position their work and say what it does, in terms collaborators from other disciplines can follow.

The problem EMI addresses is structural. Existing evaluation frameworks for artistic research were not built to capture how artistic research reorganises knowledge-making from within a collaboration. EMI is a provisional attempt to name what those frameworks miss.

The six dimensions

01
Problem Reconfiguration

Did the artistic work change how the question was framed?

Not whether art “solved” the research problem, but whether it changed what the problem was understood to be. In my account of HLaS, a question about digital health literacy as information acquisition moved toward literacy as embodied, relational and affective, with consequences for method and design.

02
Knowledge Integration

What types of knowledge were brought into contact?

Embodied, aesthetic, speculative and experiential knowledge meets empirical, clinical, social-scientific or technical knowledge. The question is not whether they integrate smoothly, which they often do not, but what becomes thinkable or perceptible through the encounter.

03
Epistemic Position & Authority

Did artistic intervention alter who could define relevance?

Authority over what counts as relevant knowledge or valid evidence is rarely equal. Artistic intervention can shift who has voice and whose experience is treated as data. This is the dimension I find most legible in the AT-LAB material.

04
Experiential Reorientation

Did collaborators or participants sense the issue differently?

Artistic research can make abstract systems perceptible, not just thinkable. In ATMOS_FERA, atmospheric data becomes a sensory environment. No audience data was collected, so this dimension is read through the design of the work and the collaboration behind it, not through audience response.

05
Evaluation Friction

Where did existing criteria fail to capture what the work was doing?

Where reviewers struggle to categorise, where funders cannot find the box, where output metrics feel beside the point: each is a trace of contribution. Friction with existing frameworks is treated as a diagnostic signal worth mapping.

06
Context & Stage Sensitivity

When and under what conditions did contribution become visible?

Contributions often become visible late, at oblique angles, or in the practice of others. At what stage of the collaboration did the artistic contribution register, and under what conditions of trust, time, shared vocabulary and institutional openness?

What EMI cannot yet claim

  • Validity beyond the two cases, in both of which I was embedded.
  • Use by anyone else. EMI has been presented and discussed; no application of it by another team is on record.
  • That the six dimensions are separate from one another, or exhaustive.
  • Any measure. No thresholds, scores or indicators in the quantitative sense; “indicator” is used loosely.
  • Any change in a collaborator’s research. For ATMOS_FERA the record supports a methodological discussion, not an effect on the scientists’ work or on an audience.

First public presentation New

On 23 April 2026 the Research Centre Art & Society at Hanze invited me to contribute to a session at Minerva Art Academy in Groningen, Exploring the role of artistic research in transdisciplinary collaboration. Researchers and representatives of three current consortia took part: Just Art, Healthy Living as a Service, and ELSA (Ethical, Legal and Social Aspects of AI). It was the first time I presented EMI in public.

The morning was chaired by Anke Coumans and opened with three short presentations. Judith van der Elst showed how artistic research is embedded in ELSA. I introduced EMI, drawing on my experience in collaborations such as HLaS, as a way of analysing and strengthening cooperation inside consortia. Bibi Straatman reflected on an epistemological paradigm shift in academia that sits close to artistic research practice. In the afternoon Miha Turšič and Zeynep Birsel of Waag Futurelab led a round of short statements from the participants.

What participants named
Openness, risk-taking, activating other ways of seeing and being, and challenging assumptions: the qualities they associate with artistic research inside a collaboration.
What worried them
The often precarious position of artistic researchers, the reduction of artistic research to “methods”, and persistent resistance within academic structures.
The discussion confirmed something that feels increasingly urgent in my work: if artistic researchers want to participate as equal partners in complex collaborations, we need stronger language for what our practices actually do, without reducing them to instrumental tools.

From my own post after the session. The organisers’ summary ends on the same point: to become equal partners in transdisciplinary consortia, artistic researchers need to articulate their practices more clearly and keep sharing strong examples of artistic research in action.

● What this shows
  • EMI has been presented in public and discussed by practitioners working in three consortia.
  • The need it addresses, a clearer language for what artistic research does inside a consortium, was echoed in the discussion and in the organisers’ summary.
! What it does not show
  • That anyone other than me can use EMI. The session was a presentation and a discussion; no application of EMI by another team is on record.
  • Anything about the six dimensions themselves. The reception on record is the organisers’ summary and my own account; structured feedback on the dimensions is not on file.

Session summary posted by Anke Coumans, Research Centre Art & Society; my own post after the session; April meeting presentation page.

Positioning EMI

EMI positions itself against existing frameworks (the S+T+ARTS Prize model, the Flemish FRIS evaluation system, the transdisciplinary-collaboration literature of Nowotny, Klein and Gibbons) and argues that none is practitioner-facing in the way required. They are institutional evaluation tools, not reflective instruments for artist-researchers working inside a collaboration.

The intended user is therefore not a funding panel. It is the artist-researcher asking: what am I doing here, epistemically? What do I contribute that my collaborators cannot? Where is the friction?
