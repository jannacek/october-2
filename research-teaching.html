Research ↔ Teaching

How the research shaped the course, how the course shaped the research, and what the teaching cannot yet show.

Four course iterations, 2023–2026 Baseline study, 10 students (2025) P+ARTS: presented · proceedings pending

The PD allocates 12.5% of its time to teaching, and the guidelines expect that teaching to connect to the candidate’s own trajectory. Here the connection is close: the course at the Frank Mohr Institute is also AT-LAB II, read as one afterwards. Teaching and research ran through each other, and the account below goes in both directions.

The course as a research environment

The course AI: In, Within, and Around Your Practice was not built to deliver findings that already existed. It was not built from the AT-LAB format, which was articulated only in 2025–26. Read back, it has the same parts: a question (how do emerging artists define, use and imagine AI?), a light way of reading what happens (a questionnaire, group dialogue, curatorial reflection) and a feed-forward into the next iteration. The students were co-inquirers in that question, not subjects of an experiment.

This question needed a live teaching situation. It could not be answered from the literature, or from work by students of someone else’s course, because the design of the encounter was part of what was being asked.

The evidence this produced, and its limits, are set out on the AT-LAB II page.

How teaching shaped the research

Concepts
Three working principles came out of teaching and were not predicted from theory: begin with conceptual clarification, embed critical literacy throughout, treat curation as research. The first of them is where the Concept Clarity lens, named in 2026 after the course, is anchored empirically. Students’ definitions of AI were heterogeneous, and that had to be handled before anything else could be discussed.
Method
Teaching forced precision. Writing the questionnaire meant saying exactly what was being asked; curating the student showcase meant developing criteria for what AI literacy in artistic practice might look like. I recognised the idea of a light reading instrument that fits the setting when I wrote up the AT-LAB form in 2025–26, and I see it again in the civic labs; I did not record it at the time as a decision.
Material
The 2025 questionnaire (ten students, taken in the first class), the curatorial reflection on the 2024 showcase and the dialogue with selected students are the empirical materials of AT-LAB II. What they show is a starting point: frequent personal use of AI, hesitant use in artistic practice, heterogeneous definitions. It is a pattern in one cohort, not a general finding.
Peer-reviewed output
The study was submitted double-blind to P+ARTS 2025 as From Conceptual Ambiguity to Critical Engagement: AI Literacy as Artistic Research, and I presented it at Unframing Knowledge in Naples on 28 October 2025. It is accepted for the proceedings, which have not yet been published, so it counts here as a presented conference contribution, not a publication.

How research shaped the teaching

The influence ran the other way as well, though less neatly than a sequence suggests. The first iteration of the course ran in June 2023, the same month as AT-LAB I, so the course did not follow from it. AT-LAB I was a sensory encounter in a health setting that lasted minutes; in my later reading it pointed to a setting where a vocabulary for AI could be built over weeks, and the course already was that setting. What is documented as a decision at the time is narrower: curation as research after the feedback meeting of 4 June 2024 (E05), and conceptual clarification as the first step after the baseline of 2025 (E18).

Read back through the format, each iteration carries a research question, its own reading instruments and a feed-forward; that is how I describe them now, since the format came afterwards. One change is documented: in 2025 the baseline questionnaire showed how much students’ definitions of AI varied, and conceptual clarification became the course’s first step.

The 2026 course is smaller, one EC in three sessions. Its outline, dated 11 March 2026 (E38), asks students to articulate a personal position toward the use or refusal of AI in their practice. The re-analysis of the AT-LAB I forms, in which refusal was the one code no returned form met, was done in June 2026, after that outline, so the provision did not follow from it. I set the two side by side because they speak to the same question, which positions people feel are open to them. That is a connection recognised later, not a decision and not an effect.

Supervision treated the two together. The feedback meeting of 4 June 2024 discussed my teaching obligations along with the research, and how students could be involved in it. The supervision record has the detail.

⚑ What the teaching cannot yet show

No change in students’ understanding is measured: the 2025 questionnaire was taken at the start, and no before-and-after comparison is claimed. The study covers ten students in one cohort of art students. The students’ own later use of the ideas has been observed informally and is not documented. No data from the 2026 iteration has been analysed. And whether the course continues without me is not established.

The coming year

The plan for the next year is targeted student supervision, not full course delivery: studio visits and individual mentoring for students whose work touches artistic research, AI and data literacy, and focused feedback moments linked to the themes of the research group Image in Context. It keeps the teaching close to the research at a smaller scale. A related line, the work on AI guidelines for higher arts education, asks the same questions at the level of institutions.
